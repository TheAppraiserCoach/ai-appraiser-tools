# Getting Paid: Invoicing as a Contractor

> **OWNER'S NOTE (never shown to the hire):** This is the 1099 version of the "Getting
> Paid" module. It replaces the W-2 timesheet module for contractor tracks. The invoicing
> details come from `payroll.contractor` in your config — set them, or it falls back to
> your employee payroll settings, which will read wrong to a contractor.

You are an independent contractor, not an employee, and that changes how money works. No
one is going to hand you a paycheck with the math already done. You invoice, you get paid
per assignment, and you handle your own taxes. This module is how that works here.

## 1. You invoice — nobody clocks you in

There is no timesheet and no time clock. You are paid a **fee per assignment**, agreed
before you start it, not an hourly wage for hours worked. If an assignment takes you twice
as long as you expected, the fee is still the fee. If you get faster, the fee is still the
fee, and the efficiency is yours to keep. That is the deal on both ends.

Confirm the fee **before** you accept an assignment. If the scope changes midway — a
property turns out to be three units instead of one, the client adds a retrospective date —
stop and get the revised fee in writing before you do the extra work. Renegotiating after
delivery almost never goes well.

waits for the next run.

Every invoice needs, at minimum:

- your name and business name, and your address
- the invoice date and an invoice number you don't reuse
- the property address or file number for each assignment
- the agreed fee for each assignment
- the total, and how you want to be paid

Keep a copy of every invoice you send. That is your business record, not the Firm's.

If an invoice hasn't been paid and the window has passed, follow up with {{owner_name}} at

quickly and old ones get sorted out slowly.

## 4. Taxes are yours

This is the part that surprises people in their first contractor year.

- **Nothing is withheld from your pay.** No federal income tax, no Social Security, no
  Medicare. The full fee lands in your account, and part of it is not yours.
- **You owe self-employment tax** in addition to income tax, because you pay both halves of
  Social Security and Medicare rather than splitting it with an employer.
- **You will likely owe quarterly estimated payments.** Missing them can mean penalties
  even if you pay in full at year end.
- **Set money aside from every payment.** Many contractors hold back roughly a quarter to a
  third of each fee in a separate account. What is right for you depends on your situation.
- **You must have a W-9 on file before your first payment** (a W-8BEN if you are not a US
  person). No form, no payment — the Firm is not allowed to skip it.
- If the Firm pays you **$600 or more** in a calendar year, you get a **1099-NEC** in
  January reporting the total. It is on you to reconcile it against your own records.

Get an accountant before your first tax season as a contractor, not after. It is the
cheapest professional advice you will ever buy.

## 5. Your expenses are yours

Mileage, your vehicle, your phone, your software, your MLS and data subscriptions, your
license and continuing education, your errors and omissions insurance — those are your
business expenses, and they come out of your fees. The Firm does not reimburse them unless
it agreed in writing, in advance, to reimburse a specific cost.

The upside: as a business, legitimate expenses reduce what you are taxed on. **Track them
all year, every year.** Mileage especially — it is usually a contractor's single largest
deduction and the easiest one to lose by not writing it down. Log the date, the purpose,
and the miles at the time, not from memory in April.

## 6. What being a contractor does not change

Independence in how you work is not independence from the standards. USPAP applies to
every assignment. The signature on the report is yours, the license on the line is yours,
and the workfile obligation is yours — and the Firm still needs its copy of the workfile so
it can meet its own retention requirement.

Being paid per assignment also never means being paid for an outcome. **No fee here is
contingent on a value, on a loan closing, or on any party being happy with the number.** If
anyone ever suggests your next assignment depends on how this one comes out, that is the
conversation you report to {{owner_name}} the same day.
