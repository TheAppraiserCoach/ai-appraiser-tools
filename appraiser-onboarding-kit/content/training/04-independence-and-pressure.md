# Independence, Pressure, and Bias

This is the most important module in this training. Everything else is craft. This is the
part that ends careers when it goes wrong — and the part that protects you when you get it
right.

## 1. What appraiser independence means

Your opinion of value is yours. It is based on the data and your analysis, and it does not
move because someone wants it to.

This isn't only ethics — it's federal law. Appraiser independence requirements built on
Dodd-Frank make it unlawful to bribe, coerce, extort, collude with, instruct, induce, or
otherwise cause an appraiser to base a value on anything but their independent judgment.
Those protections exist **for** you. Use them.

## 2. What pressure actually sounds like

It is almost never "change the number." It's softer, and it usually comes from someone
pleasant who has a deadline.

- "We just need it to come in at {{example_pressure_value}}, can you make that work?"
- "The contract price is X — will you have any trouble getting there?"
- "The homeowner says you missed the finished basement." (When you didn't.)
- "The last appraiser had no problem with this."
- "If this doesn't work out we'll have to use someone else next time."
- "Can you just take another look at these comps?" — with a list attached, all higher.
- "Don't put that in the report, it'll kill the loan."

Some of these are legitimate. A client **is** allowed to ask you to consider additional
comps, to correct a factual error, to provide more support for your conclusion, or to
explain your reasoning. That's a reconsideration of value, and it's a normal part of the
business.

**The line is simple:** being given new information and asked to consider it is legitimate.
Being told what conclusion to reach is not. Comps you consider and reject — with your
reasoning documented — is you doing your job. Comps you use because someone insisted is
you losing your license.

## 3. What you do about it

1. **Don't change your opinion because of the pressure.** Consider new information on its
   merits, every time. Then reach the conclusion the data supports.
2. **Document it.** Who said it, when, and as close to their exact words as you can manage.
   Put it in the workfile. A contemporaneous note is worth enormously more than a memory
   two years later.
3. **Escalate it.** Tell {{owner_name}} promptly — the same day if it was explicit.

You will not be retaliated against here for refusing to change a value, for reporting
pressure, or for reporting a suspected violation. If the pressure ever comes from inside
this office, that is exactly the situation the escalation path exists for.

## 4. Things you may never do

- State or imply a **predetermined value**, or accept an assignment conditioned on one.
- Accept a **contingent fee** — one tied to the value reached, the direction of value, or
  whether a loan closes.
- Agree to a **minimum valuation** as a condition of getting work.
- Change a supported conclusion because a party is unhappy with it.

If an assignment arrives with any of these attached, don't negotiate it — bring it to
{{owner_name}}.

## 5. Bias and fair housing

An appraisal that is influenced by protected characteristics is both an ethics violation
and a violation of federal law — the Fair Housing Act and ECOA. This has been an area of
intense regulatory focus, and the standards for what's acceptable in a report have
tightened.

**Never** base any part of an analysis or a conclusion on race, color, religion, national
origin, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, familial status, age, disability, or the
perceived composition of a neighborhood.

Watch your language in reports. Avoid describing neighborhoods or occupants in terms that
reference or proxy for protected characteristics. Terms like "desirable area," "pride of
ownership," "changing neighborhood," or comments about schools, crime, or "the type of
people" are the classic traps — some are subjective, some function as proxies, and none of
them belong in a report. **Describe the market with data, not with impressions.**

Practical habits that keep you clean:

- Support neighborhood and market conclusions with actual data — sales, days on market,
  trends — not adjectives.
- Choose comparables using objective similarity: location, physical characteristics, market
  behavior. Be able to state why each one is in and why obvious alternatives are out.
- Don't let who owns or occupies a property, or what's displayed inside it, influence
  anything.
- If you catch yourself making an assumption you couldn't support with data, that's the
  assumption to remove.

## 6. If you get it wrong

If you realize a report has an error — a measurement, a comp, a conclusion you can no
longer support — say so immediately. Errors get corrected. Concealed errors become
misleading reports, and a misleading report is a different and much more serious thing.

# Independence and Bias Quiz (6 questions)

**Q1. A loan officer emails: "The contract is $415,000 — will you have any trouble getting there?" What is this?**
- A) A normal request for a status update
- B) A legitimate reconsideration of value
- C) ✅ Pressure toward a predetermined value — don't let it move your opinion, document it, and report it
- D) A request you should ignore without recording

**Q2. A client sends three additional comparable sales and asks you to consider them. What's the correct response?**
- A) Refuse — accepting comps from a client is prohibited
- B) Use them, since the client knows their market
- C) ✅ Consider them on their merits, use or reject each based on the data, and document your reasoning
- D) Consider them only if they support your existing conclusion

**Q3. Which fee arrangement is unlawful and prohibited?**
- A) A higher fee for a complex property
- B) A flat fee set before the assignment
- C) A rush fee for a short turnaround
- D) ✅ A fee that depends on the value you reach or on whether the loan closes

**Q4. Which of these belongs in an appraisal report?**
- A) "This is a desirable neighborhood with pride of ownership"
- B) "The area is changing"
- C) ✅ "Median days on market in the subject's market area declined from 46 to 31 over the past 12 months"
- D) "Good schools make this area attractive to the right kind of buyer"

**Q5. You realize after delivery that you used a comparable with the wrong square footage, and it affects your conclusion. What do you do?**
- A) Leave it — the report is already delivered
- B) Fix it quietly in the workfile only
- C) ✅ Report it immediately so it can be corrected — concealed errors turn into misleading reports
- D) Wait to see whether anyone catches it

**Q6. Someone at this office pressures you to change a supported value. What's the right move?**
- A) Change it — they're senior to you
- B) Refuse but say nothing further
- C) Ask the client to decide
- D) ✅ Don't change it, document exactly what was said, and escalate it — the escalation path exists for this exact situation
