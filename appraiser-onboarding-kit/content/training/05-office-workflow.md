# The Assembly Line: How Work Moves Through This Office

This office does not run appraisals as one person doing everything start to finish. It runs
an **assembly line**: the work is broken into small, defined steps, each step is done by the
right level of person, and the file moves down the line with a handoff at every transfer.

This is how a small office produces consistent quality at volume, and it's how you'll learn
the trade — by mastering steps, then earning harder ones. Read this module to understand
the line and where you fit on day one.

## 1. The three tiers

Every step on the line is tagged with the skill level it requires:

- **Tier I — Office / administrative.** No appraisal license or judgment required: saving
  and logging orders, scheduling, calendar, copying checklists, sending handoff emails,
  uploading and downloading files. Precision matters; valuation judgment doesn't.
- **Tier II — Skilled production.** Trained, repeatable work with some judgment: quoting
  and accepting orders, pulling MLS sheets and history, county tax data, GIS/plat maps,
  zoning, deed and transfer info, inputting comparables, syncing reports on and off the
  field device, placing maps, delivering reports to the client portal.
- **Tier III — Appraiser judgment.** The licensed core of the job: choosing comparables,
  the inspection itself, adjustments and their support, reconciliation and narrative,
  quality control, and substantive revisions. Only appropriately licensed or supervised
  people do Tier III work — and Tier III time is the most expensive time in the office,
  which is why the line exists: to keep it spent only on what actually requires it.

**The rule of the line: work at your tier, hand off between tiers.** A Tier III appraiser
typing county data into a form is wasting the client's turnaround time. A Tier I assistant
guessing at a comparable is worse. Both problems have the same cause — someone doing a step
that isn't theirs.

## 2. The line, in phases

The full line is about three dozen steps. You'll get the printed step list with exact times
in your first week; here's the shape of it:

**Phase 1 — Intake (Tiers I–II).** Quote the potential order, accept it, save it into the
digital workfile, log it in the office management system.

**Phase 2 — Scheduling (Tier I).** Call and schedule the inspection, put it on the
calendar, update the client with the inspection date. The client is told promptly — an
unscheduled order going quiet is how offices lose clients.

**Phase 3 — Data gathering (Tiers I–II).** Copy the data-gathering checklist, then pull
everything the analysis will need: MLS sheets and history, county tax data, GIS/plat map,
zoning and dimensions, deed/transfer info from the county. Then the handoff email goes to
the appraiser or trainee: the file is ready for comparable selection.

**Phase 4 — Build and inspect (all three tiers).** The appraiser **chooses comps**
(Tier III). Photos of comps are arranged. The report shell is built in the appraisal
software from the office template — sketch started where possible — comps are input, and
the built report is loaded onto the appraiser's mobile device. Then the **inspection**:
sketching, photos of subject and comps, and data collection at the property. From the
field, the inspected report is uploaded back to the office the same day.

**Phase 5 — Write-up and QC (Tiers I–III).** The office downloads the inspected report,
runs the unsync checklist to move the field data back into the desktop file, and emails the
appraiser that the file is ready for write-up. The appraiser does **adjustments and their
support**, then **reconciliation and narrative** (Tier III). Maps go in (Tier II). Then the
**supervisor's quality-control check** (Tier III) — every report, every time, no matter who
wrote it — and any errors found get fixed before anything leaves the building.

**Phase 6 — Delivery and revisions (Tiers I–III).** Copy the upload checklist, upload the
final report to the client. When revision requests come back: minor fixes are Tier II,
substantive ones are Tier III (and anything touching value follows the reconsideration
rules from Module 4). The revised report is re-uploaded, and the file goes to retention.

## 3. Checklists are not optional

You'll notice the line copies a fresh checklist at four points — data gathering, report
build, unsync, and upload. That's deliberate. The checklist is how a 36-step process
produces the same quality on a Tuesday afternoon in October as it did the day we designed
it.

- **Use the checklist every time**, including when you've done the step a hundred times.
  The checklist exists precisely because experienced people skip steps from memory.
- **A fresh copy per assignment**, checked off as you go — not at the end from memory.
- If a checklist item doesn't fit the assignment, you don't silently skip it — you note
  why. And if you think the checklist itself is wrong, say so; improving the line is
  everyone's job. Changing it unilaterally is nobody's.

## 4. Handoffs: the email is the baton

The line moves by explicit handoffs — "file ready for comps," "report ready for write-up,"
"ready for QC." The handoff email (or task, depending on the office system) is the baton.

- **No silent handoffs.** Finishing your step means the next person *knows* it's their
  turn. Work that sits because "I finished my part and figured they'd see it" is the #1
  way turnaround dies.
- **No half handoffs.** If your step isn't actually complete — a document missing, a photo
  set partial — the baton doesn't pass. Flag what's missing instead of passing a problem
  downstream with your name on it.
- **Same-day field uploads.** The inspected report leaves the field the day of the
  inspection. Field data sitting on a device overnight is risk with no upside.

## 5. Where you start, and how you grow

You'll start with Tier I steps and learn them until they're boringly reliable. That's not
hazing — it's how you learn the whole line: Tier I touches every phase, so you'll see how
an order becomes a delivered report before you're responsible for the judgment inside it.

From there, growth is explicit: you'll be trained on specific Tier II steps and assigned
them formally. Tier III comes with licensure and supervision, per your state's rules and
Module 1's scope rule: **never do a step above your assigned tier without checking first —
and never let a step assigned to you sit undone without flagging it.**

Time matters on every step — most steps on the line have a target time in minutes, and the
whole line is measured. If a step consistently takes you far longer than its target, that's
a training conversation, not a secret to keep. If you find a way to do a step *better or
faster without losing quality*, that's exactly the kind of thing to bring up.
