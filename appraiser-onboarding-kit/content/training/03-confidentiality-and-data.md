# Confidentiality and Handling Data

Appraisal work runs on other people's private information. You will see what a house looks
like inside, what someone paid for it, what they still owe, sometimes their financial
situation, and often their name, phone number, and daily schedule. Handling that carefully
is part of the job, not an IT policy tacked onto it.

## 1. What is confidential

Two categories, and USPAP treats them differently.

**Confidential information** — information the client identifies as confidential, plus
information that isn't publicly available that you got from the client. Example: the
client's instructions, the fee arrangement, the borrower's file details.

**Assignment results** — your opinions and conclusions, including the value itself.
Assignment results are confidential regardless of whether anyone labeled them that way.

You may disclose these to: **the client**, parties the client specifically authorizes,
state enforcement agencies and third parties as authorized by law, and a duly authorized
professional peer review committee. That list is short on purpose. Everyone else is "no."

## 2. The people who will ask you, and the answer

You will be asked for the value. Constantly. Usually by people who seem perfectly entitled
to it.

- **The homeowner**, during or after the inspection. Even if they're the borrower. Even if
  they're paying for the loan. If the lender is the client, the homeowner is not — the
  answer is "the report goes to the lender, and they'll share it with you."
- **The real estate agent** who let you in, or who calls afterward "just checking how it
  came in."
- **The loan officer** who wants a heads-up before the report lands.
- **Friends and family**, about a house they know you appraised.

The universal answer: *"I can't discuss it — the report goes to my client."* You are not
being rude, and you are not required to explain the rule to them. Say it and move on.

**Know who the client actually is** before you talk to anyone. On a lender or AMC
assignment, the client is the lender or the AMC — not the homeowner, not the agent, and not
whoever paid the fee.

## 3. Personal information

Borrower and homeowner personal information — names, addresses, phone numbers, financial
details, anything nonpublic — gets protected like it's your own.

- Don't email it unencrypted to people who don't need it, and check the recipient line
  before you hit send. Misdirected email is the single most common way this goes wrong.
- Don't store files on personal devices, personal email, or a personal cloud account. Firm
  files live in Firm systems.
- Don't leave printed files, notes, or a laptop visible in your vehicle. A file left on a
  passenger seat is a data breach waiting for a broken window.
- Lock your devices. Use a unique password for Firm accounts and turn on two-factor
  authentication where it's offered.
- When a file's retention period ends, disposal is handled by the office, not by you.

## 4. Photographs

You photograph what the assignment requires — the property, its condition, and its
features.

You do not photograph, and you delete if you catch it: documents lying out, computer
screens, mail, prescription bottles, safes, firearms, children, and people generally.
Photos live in the workfile, and the workfile can be reviewed by people you'll never meet.

If a homeowner asks you not to photograph a specific room, note the request, photograph
what you can, and tell {{owner_name}} — an unusual restriction may affect the assignment.

## 5. Social media and talking shop

Do not post about specific assignments. Not the address, not the photos, not "you would not
believe the house I was in today." Appraisers have lost their licenses over posts they
thought were anonymous — a distinctive interior is identifiable, and the person who
recognizes it is often the homeowner.

Talking about the work in general terms — the market, the profession, a technique — is
fine. The line is the specific property, the specific client, and the specific assignment.

## 6. When you think something went wrong

Tell {{owner_name}} the same day. A misdirected email, a lost phone, a file left
somewhere, a conversation you shouldn't have had. Early disclosure usually makes it a
manageable problem; a delay usually makes it a serious one. You will not be punished for
reporting your own mistake promptly.

# Confidentiality Quiz (5 questions)

**Q1. You finish an inspection and the homeowner asks what the value is going to be. The lender is the client. What do you say?**
- A) Give them a range so they're not blindsided
- B) Tell them only if they're the one paying the appraisal fee
- C) ✅ Tell them you can't discuss it and the report goes to your client, the lender
- D) Tell them after the report is delivered

**Q2. Which group may assignment results be disclosed to?**
- A) Anyone who asks in writing
- B) The homeowner, always, since it's their house
- C) ✅ The client, parties the client authorizes, state enforcement agencies and third parties as authorized by law, and a duly authorized professional peer review committee
- D) The real estate agents on both sides of the transaction

**Q3. Where should Firm appraisal files be stored?**
- A) Wherever is most convenient, as long as they're backed up
- B) On your personal cloud account so you can reach them in the field
- C) ✅ In Firm systems only — not on personal devices, personal email, or personal cloud accounts
- D) On a USB drive you keep in your vehicle

**Q4. While photographing a bedroom you notice financial documents spread out on the desk. What do you do?**
- A) Photograph the room as-is — you need the documentation
- B) Photograph it and crop later
- C) ✅ Don't photograph the documents; capture what the assignment requires and leave them out
- D) Ask the homeowner to sign a release

**Q5. You realize you emailed a report to the wrong address. What's the right move?**
- A) Recall the email and say nothing if the recall succeeds
- B) Wait to see if anyone responds before raising it
- C) ✅ Tell {{owner_name}} the same day — prompt reporting usually keeps it manageable
- D) Send a follow-up asking the recipient to delete it, and handle it yourself
